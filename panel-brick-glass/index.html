Strengths and weaknesses

What each one is good at

Panelka

Large-panel prefab · 1959–1990s

Strengths

  • Cheapest and fastest of the three, by a wide margin
  • Rigid box of walls copes well with earthquakes
  • Structure often outlasts its design life
  • Housed tens of millions of families who had nowhere else to live

Weaknesses

  • Panel joints leak air and water, causing cold bridges and mould
  • You can hear the neighbours, their footsteps and their plumbing
  • Low 2.5 m ceilings, small kitchens
  • Walls are structural, so layouts are fixed

Best when retrofitted with external insulation, new windows and resealed joints.

Brick block

Load-bearing masonry · 1930s–1950s

Strengths

  • Lasts a century or more with basic care
  • High ceilings and generous rooms
  • Heavy walls block sound and keep summers cool
  • Lowest embodied carbon of the three

Weaknesses

  • Slow and labour-intensive to build
  • Thick walls eat floor area, and still lose heat
  • Prone to damp; old timber floors decay
  • Unreinforced brick is brittle in earthquakes

Best when kept dry, repointed and fitted with modern windows and roof insulation.

Glass & panel

Concrete frame + curtain wall · 2000s–today

Strengths

  • Free plans: only columns and the core are fixed
  • Insulated panels make the best walls of the three
  • Lots of daylight and views; can go very tall
  • Ductile frames ride out earthquakes when built to code

Weaknesses

  • Overheats in sun and depends on air conditioning
  • The glass skin wears out in 20–40 years
  • Most expensive, highest embodied carbon
  • Noise can creep along slab edges and light partitions

Best when glass is limited to where it earns its place, with external shading and solar-control coatings.

Notes and sources

Figures are typical ranges for buildings as originally built, rounded for teaching. Build speeds, sound values, cost indices and embodied-carbon ranges are indicative engineering estimates, not measurements of any one building. The summer temperatures in chapter 5 come from a simple illustrative model, not from measurements. All three buildings are drawn at nine storeys so they can be compared like for like. Their heights differ only because their storey heights differ.

  • Panelka design life is quoted as both 25 and 50 years. Neither figure is well documented. A Belarusian study of the 1-464A series found spare structural capacity and 20–25 years of extra life if the joints are resealed (BSTU Herald).
  • Large-panel buildings generally performed well in the 1988 Spitak earthquake, credited to their rigidity and redundancy. Precast concrete frame buildings and old unreinforced masonry suffered most of the collapses (World Housing Encyclopedia). Minor joint cracking was reported in panel blocks.
  • Stalinka walls and ceilings: Wikipedia, “Stalinka”. Curtain-wall U-values: manufacturer data for aluminium systems with double and triple glazing.
  • Embodied carbon boundaries differ between studies. The EU whole-life baseline averages around 600 kgCO₂e/m² (Aalborg University for the EU).
